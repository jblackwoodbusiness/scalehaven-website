PLASTIC SURGERY MARKETING

Plastic Surgery Website Design That Books Consults

By John Blackwood, Founder · {{TODAY_HUMAN}} · 9 min read

A plastic surgery website has one job that no other page in your marketing can do: it is the consult coordinator who never sleeps. A prospective patient researching rhinoplasty at 11pm is not going to call. She is going to read your procedure page, study your before-and-afters, look up your credentials, and decide in about ninety seconds whether you are on her shortlist. Most surgical websites lose her in that window, not because they are ugly, but because they were designed to look impressive rather than to book.

This guide is written from the operator side. Before ScaleHaven, our founder ran the marketing inside a cosmetic clinic that grew into one of the largest in its region and sold to private equity. What follows are the twelve elements that separate plastic surgery websites that generate consults from the ones that merely exist, plus honest numbers on what a build should cost.

Why Surgical Websites Have to Work Harder

A med spa patient might book a $300 facial on impulse. A rhinoplasty patient is deciding whether to let you operate on her face for $8,000 to $15,000, and she will research for weeks. That changes what the website has to do. It has to build surgeon trust, answer detailed procedure questions, prove results within compliance rules, and make the next step feel small. Every element below serves one of those four jobs.

The 12 Elements of a Plastic Surgery Website That Converts

1. Surgeon authority above the fold

Board certification, training pedigree, hospital privileges, years in practice, society memberships. This is not vanity; it is the first thing a surgical patient checks. Put the surgeon’s name and face on the homepage, and give every surgeon a full bio page with a real photograph, not a stock headshot. Patients book surgeons, not practices.

2. One dedicated page per procedure

A single “Procedures” page listing twenty services is the most common failure in surgical web design. Each procedure needs its own URL with candidacy, technique options, recovery timeline, risks, pricing range, FAQs, and a consult call to action. This is also the architecture that ranks: our plastic surgery SEO guide shows why procedure pages carry the whole organic strategy.

3. Before-and-after galleries built for compliance

Results are the most persuasive content you own and the fastest way to get into trouble. Galleries need documented patient consent for every image, consistent lighting and angles, no retouching, and no language implying a guaranteed outcome. Organize by procedure, tag by age range and body type where consent allows, and never lift photos from manufacturers. If you run paid ads, remember that Meta restricts before-and-after imagery, so the gallery lives on your site, not in your ads.

4. A consult request form that asks for less

Name, email, phone, procedure of interest, preferred contact time. That is the whole form. Every extra field drops completion. Qualification happens on the phone with your coordinator, not on the website. The form should route to your coordinator’s inbox the second it is submitted, because the practice that responds first wins the case. That routing model is the core of our plastic surgery lead generation approach.

5. Financing, stated plainly

CareCredit, PatientFi, Alphaeon, in-house plans, whatever you offer: put it on every procedure page and in the main navigation. A $9,000 tummy tuck becomes a $240-a-month decision, and patients who see financing early convert at higher rates. Hiding it until the consult leaves money on the table.

6. Virtual consult and click-to-call on mobile

Most surgical research now starts on a phone. A sticky mobile bar with “Call” and “Request Consult” buttons outperforms any hero banner. Offering a virtual consultation lowers the barrier for out-of-town patients, who are a meaningful share of demand for surgeons with a strong reputation.

7. Speed under two seconds

Surgical sites are image-heavy, and image-heavy sites are slow. Compress every gallery image to WebP, lazy-load below the fold, and avoid page builders that ship a megabyte of scripts. Google measures this as Core Web Vitals, and it is a ranking factor, but the bigger cost is human: a page that takes four seconds loses a third of its visitors before it renders.

8. Real photography of the practice

Your building, your consult rooms, your operating suite if accredited, your staff. Patients want to see where the surgery happens. Stock photography of models in white robes signals that the practice has nothing real to show.

9. Reviews woven into the page, not parked on one

Pull Google and RealSelf reviews onto procedure pages next to the relevant results. A rhinoplasty review belongs on the rhinoplasty page. A separate “Testimonials” page that nobody visits does not move consults.

10. Surgeon video

A two-minute video of the surgeon explaining how they approach a procedure does more for trust than ten pages of copy. Patients choose people, and video is the closest thing to meeting you. Phone quality with good light is fine; authenticity outperforms production value in aesthetics every time.

11. Local SEO and schema

Physician and MedicalProcedure structured data, a Google Business Profile that matches the site exactly, and location pages if you serve more than one metro. “Plastic surgeon near me” is a five-figure monthly search; the map pack decides half of it.

12. Tracking that tells you what booked

GA4 with consult form submissions and calls as key events, call tracking on the main number, and a monthly look at which pages produced consults. Without this, every redesign decision is a guess. Ask any agency pitching you to show how they will measure booked consults, not traffic.

Real Result

For a Texas med spa we ran Google and Meta together from day one, with named offers and every lead routed straight to the front desk, who called it back. $2,500 in ad spend came back as $28,000 in first-visit revenue in the first 30 days, about $50 to acquire each new patient. No new software, no integrations: the leads landed in the booking system the clinic already used.

What Plastic Surgery Website Design Costs in 2026

Rough market ranges. A template build on Squarespace or WordPress with light customization runs $3,000 to $8,000. A custom agency build from the large aesthetic-marketing firms runs $15,000 to $40,000 and often comes bundled with hosting and marketing retainers you cannot leave without losing the site. Our own conversion-first build is a one-time $3,500 USD, you own the domain and the files, and it is designed around the twelve elements above rather than around an awards submission.

Two questions to ask any builder before signing: who owns the domain, hosting, and code when the contract ends, and how many of their previous surgical sites can they show with real consult numbers behind them? The first protects you from lock-in. The second separates designers from marketers.

A great site with no traffic books nobody. In our four-pillar visibility system, the website is pillar one because everything else, SEO, Google Ads, and Meta, sends patients to it. Build it to convert first, then turn on the channels. Practices that do it in the other order pay for clicks that leak. Frequently Asked Questions

How much should a plastic surgeon spend on a website?

Between $3,500 and $15,000 buys a professional, conversion-focused site in 2026. Above that you are usually paying for agency overhead or a bundled retainer, not for more consults. Spend the savings on photography and surgeon video, which move conversion more than design flourishes.

Can plastic surgeons show before-and-after photos on their website?

Yes, with documented patient consent for every image, no retouching, and no wording that implies guaranteed results. State medical boards and the FTC both police outcome claims. The rules are stricter in paid social ads, where Meta restricts before-and-after imagery, which is one more reason the gallery belongs on your own site.

What platform is best for a plastic surgery website?

The platform matters less than ownership and speed. WordPress, Webflow, and well-built static sites all work. Avoid any platform where leaving means losing your content, and avoid heavy page builders that make image-rich procedure pages slow on mobile.

How long does a plastic surgery website take to build?

Four to eight weeks for a focused build once photography and content are ready. The content is usually the bottleneck: procedure pages need surgeon input, and galleries need consent paperwork. Start those before the design work begins.
